I started out reverse engineering malware, and nine years later I still spend my days pulling things apart — the targets have just gotten more interesting. I direct several security teams at Ebryx, in Lahore, and the throughline across all of them is the same: understand a system well enough to break it, then use that understanding to build something that resists breaking.

My path ran through reverse engineering and vulnerability research before it got to leadership. At FireEye in Singapore I spent three years hunting evasions that bypassed our IDS, doing root-cause analysis on live detections, and building a MITRE ATT&CK mapping into the product UI. At Ensign InfoSecurity, I reverse-engineered a corporate security product's C++ internals — reconstructing object hierarchies, debugging Windows kernel drivers and minifilters — to find where its own security assumptions broke down. That reverse-engineering background isn't legacy experience I've moved past; it's the reason the rest of the work holds up.

At Ebryx I now direct teams across Linux kernel research (exploit primitive development, adapting public PoCs to live kernel versions), red team tooling (including a custom C2 framework and payload delivery mechanisms), a rotating vulnerability research group that has landed several public CVEs, and — the newest and sharpest edge — agentic AI security: tool-use boundaries, agent-to-agent trust modeling, memory isolation, and the guardrails that keep an autonomous agent from doing something nobody authorized.

That last one is the part I think is underrated. Most people approaching agent security come from an ML background and treat it as a prompting problem. It isn't. Privilege boundaries, trust across untrusted inputs, memory isolation, sandbox escape — these are old exploitation problems wearing a new runtime, and I've spent a decade on the old version.
